research

Studying the Higgs boson with the CMS experiment at the Large Hadron Collider.

Our group works on the CMS experiment at the CERN Large Hadron Collider (LHC), where protons collide at the highest energies ever reached in a laboratory. We use those collisions to study the Higgs boson, the particle tied to the origin of mass, and to ask whether it behaves exactly as the Standard Model predicts.

Two themes run through our work: how the Higgs boson interacts with itself, and how it interacts with the lighter matter particles.

Higgs self-coupling

Higgs boson pairs in the four-b final state

The shape of the Higgs field's potential is one of the least tested parts of the Standard Model. Producing two Higgs bosons at once is the most direct way to probe it, because the rate depends on how a Higgs boson couples to itself. Each Higgs boson decays most often to a pair of bottom quarks, so we search for events with four b-quark jets, a signal that is rare and buried under enormous backgrounds. The work combines jet reconstruction, b-quark identification, and machine learning to pull out that signal, including the case where a Higgs boson is so energetic that its decay products merge into a single jet.

Read the CMS paper (arXiv:2604.27044)

Higgs and the muon

Higgs boson decay to two muons

Why do the matter particles have such different masses? The Higgs boson couples to each of them, and the muon is the lightest second-generation particle we can study this way. The Higgs boson decays to a muon pair only very rarely, but muons are measured cleanly in CMS, so the decay appears as a narrow peak in the dimuon mass spectrum. Finding it means separating a small bump from a smooth background, and sorting events by how the Higgs boson was produced to gain sensitivity. It is a good example of how careful statistics and detector understanding turn a faint signal into a measurement.

Read the CMS paper (arXiv:2009.04363)
